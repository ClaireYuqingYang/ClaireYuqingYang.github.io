## Abstract

Social media platforms blur the boundaries between information, entertainment, and social interaction, creating conditions under which misinformation spreads. The current research argues that misinformation sharing is driven partly by competition between users’ accuracy goals and other sharing goals. Existing accuracy interventions ask users to set non-accuracy-oriented goals aside in order to prioritize accuracy, and typically prompt accuracy before or between browsing sessions rather than keeping it visible throughout browsing. We propose that interventions can instead reduce misinformation sharing by making accuracy compatible with users’ other sharing goals and keeping those goals visible as users scroll. We introduce Accuracy+, a goal-setting intervention in which users set an accuracy goal together with another sharing goal of their choice (e.g., making people laugh), which remain visible in a banner at the top of their feed. A pilot study (N = 598) shows that a persistent banner reminding users of their accuracy goal improves sharing discernment beyond eliciting that goal once. We preregister an experiment on DICE, a simulated social media feed, comparing Accuracy+, with and without an implementation plan, to an accuracy goal and a control. We hypothesize that Accuracy+ will improve sharing discernment by reducing sharing of lower-quality relative to higher-quality posts. The findings will advance a goal-competition theory of misinformation sharing and offer platforms a scalable, user-directed intervention that empowers users to set and stick to their own goals, preserving their autonomy and the goals that drive engagement.
